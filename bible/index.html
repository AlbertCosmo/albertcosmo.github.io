Bible · Comparative reading

Kneaded, Beaten, and Unbowed

Women’s agency in the Song of Songs and Hồ Xuân Hương’s “Bánh trôi nước”

Albert Le Thanh Nhan
The Bible as Literature · Prof. Koss · 30 October 2026

2

The question

Two women say “I” inside systems that dispose of their bodies

Song of Songs

A young woman answers to brothers, watchmen, and a king’s name.

“Bánh trôi nước”

A woman speaking as a rice cake answers to fate, water, and an unnamed kẻ nặn, “the one who kneads.”

Agency here means less the power to escape constraint than the capacity to speak of oneself, to want, to act, and to claim possession of oneself in words.

3

Thesis

One speech act, two directions

Both poems locate agency in a final speech act of self-possession that answers the hands that hold the woman. The two speech acts point in opposite directions.

Outward and erotic. The Song’s woman seeks, seizes, leads, and asks to be set as a seal on another’s heart. She pays for her mobility in the city’s violence.
Inward and ethical. Xuân Hương’s speaker cannot move of her own accord, but she keeps her heart.

The Song lets its woman desire; the quatrain lets its woman remain.

4

5

Part I · The poet

Hồ Xuân Hương, “the Queen of Nôm poetry”

  • Usually dated 1772–1822, though the facts of her life are hard to verify.
  • Family from Quỳnh Đôi, Nghệ An; raised in Thăng Long (Hà Nội), near West Lake.
  • Married twice, by most accounts as a concubine (vợ lẽ).
  • Xuân Diệu called her Bà chúa thơ Nôm, “the Queen of Nôm poetry.”
  • In 2022, after a UNESCO resolution, Việt Nam marked 250 years since her birth and 200 since her death.

Part I · The poems in print

From hand copies to French and English

  1. 19th centuryHer Nôm poems circulate in hand copies.
  2. 1909 · 1914First woodblock printings, including Quốc âm thi tuyển (1914).
  3. 1968 · FrenchMaurice Durand (1914–1966): Nôm texts, translation and notes, published by the École française d’Extrême-Orient after his death.
  4. 2000 · EnglishJohn Balaban, Spring Essence: by his account, the first typographic printing of Nôm.

Scholars attribute anywhere from 25 to 148 poems to her, so this presentation speaks of “the speaker,” not the poet’s life.

7

Part I · The cake

Bánh trôi nước: the “cake that floats in water”

  • A ball of glutinous rice dough around a cube of brown sugar.
  • Dropped into boiling water, it sinks at first; it is done when it floats.
  • Eaten on Cold Food Day (Tết Hàn thực), the 3rd day of the 3rd lunar month.

The kitchen supplies every image in the poem: white and round, floating and sinking, kneaded by hand, with a dark sweet heart inside.

Part I · Reading the quatrain

Words that carry the argument

身 · 分thân · phậnbody · lot: white body, round fate
𠤩浽𠀧沉bảy nổi ba chìm“seven floatings, three sinkings”: life’s ups and downs
渃𡽫nước non“waters and mountains”: the homeland
默油 · 几揑mặc dầu · kẻ nặn“no matter” · “the one who kneads”
𦓡 · 刎mà · vẫn“yet” · “still”: the turn
𡨹 · 𬌓𢚸𣘈giữ · tấm lòng son“to keep” · “a vermilion heart”

Variant The school text reads “Thân em vừa trắng lại vừa tròn, / Bảy nổi ba chìm với nước non”, placing both whiteness and roundness in the body. The Nôm graph 買 can stand for more than one word: mấy (“so many”) or với (“with”).

10

Part II · Speaking the body

“Black, but comely” and “white and round”

“I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon. Look not upon me, because I am black, because the sun hath looked upon me: my mother’s children were angry with me; they made me the keeper of the vineyards; but mine own vineyard have I not kept.”Song of Sol. 1.5–6
Thân em thì trắng, phận em tròn, / Bảy nổi ba chìm mấy nước non.“My body is white, my lot is round, / seven times floating, three times sinking, through so many waters and mountains.”“Bánh trôi nước,” lines 1–2

The Song She names who caused her darkness: the sun and her brothers. The vineyard is also her own body.

The quatrain What makes her float and sink is impersonal: fate, water, nước non (the homeland).

11

Part III · Desire in motion

The Song gives its woman a chain of active verbs

“By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth . . . I will rise now, and go about the city in the streets . . . I found him whom my soul loveth: I held him, and would not let him go, until I had brought him into my mother’s house, and into the chamber of her that conceived me.”Song of Sol. 3.1–4

The Song’s woman acts

sought→rise→go about→found→held→brought

Xuân Hương’s speaker is acted upon, until the last line

nổi · floats (the water)chìm · sinks (the water)nặn · kneads (the hands)→giữ · keeps (she)

She leads him to her mother’s house, not the “father’s house,” the usual biblical term for a household, and so places her desire inside a female line of descent.

12

Part III · The cost of motion

The second night search

“I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone . . . The watchmen that went about the city found me, they smote me, they wounded me; the keepers of the walls took away my veil from me.”Song of Sol. 5.6–7
  1. 5.7The watchmen who merely “found” her in chapter 3 now beat her, wound her, and strip her veil.
  2. 5.8She charges the daughters of Jerusalem to tell her lover “that I am sick of love.”
  3. 5.10–16She gives the Song’s one extended description of a man’s body, seen by a woman.

The beating interrupts her search, but it does not end her speech. Xuân Hương’s speaker never reaches the street, and what she keeps is inside her, where no watchman can take it.

13

Part IV · Walls and vineyards

“I am a wall”: answering the brothers

“We have a little sister, and she hath no breasts: what shall we do for our sister in the day when she shall be spoken for? If she be a wall, we will build upon her a palace of silver: and if she be a door, we will inclose her with boards of cedar.”Song of Sol. 8.8–9

Brothers Third person: their sister is property to prepare for a marriage negotiation. A wall will be decorated; a door will be boarded shut.

She First person: “I am a wall, and my breasts like towers” (8.10). She takes their metaphor and turns it into her own fortification.

1.6mine own vineyard have I not kept
→
8.12My vineyard, which is mine, is before me
14

Part IV · Concession and reply

“Mà em vẫn giữ”: yet I still keep

Rắn nát mặc dầu tay kẻ nặn, / Mà em vẫn giữ tấm lòng son.“Firm or crushed, whatever the kneader’s hands may make of me, / yet I still keep my vermilion heart.”“Bánh trôi nước,” lines 3–4

Line 3 concedes everything external, as a Vietnamese woman of her time was expected to. The speaker admits the kneader’s power, which sets the stage for her reply: her noble character does not waver.

The Song’s woman reclaims her vineyard, which is to say her body, and places it before her own eyes.
Xuân Hương’s speaker gives her body up to the kneading hands and reclaims only what is inside.

Her agency costs her a split between body and heart that the Song’s woman does not have to accept.

15

Part V · The seal and the vermilion heart

One woman asks to be the seal; the other keeps the vermilion

“Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death . . . Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it: if a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned.”Song of Sol. 8.6–7

The seal A personal mark of identity, worn on a cord or on the hand. She asks to be pressed onto her lover’s heart, to mark him as hers. A woman prepared for a marriage negotiation ends by saying that love has no price.

The vermilion Tấm lòng son (𬌓𢚸𣘈) joins the heart to son, the red of the paste in which East Asian seals are stamped.

The water Many waters cannot quench love; the cake floats seven times and sinks three, and her heart survives.

16

Synthesis

Two kinds of agency grown under control

The Song’s womanXuân Hương’s speaker
The handsBrothers, watchmen, a king’s nameFate, water, the kneader
ModeErotic, outwardEthical, inward
Signature actsSeeks, seizes, leads, answers, marks him as hersFloats and sinks, is kneaded, keeps
The costThe city punishes her mobilityThe unity of body and heart
Last word“My vineyard, which is mine, is before me”“Mà em vẫn giữ tấm lòng son”

Neither woman escapes the hands that claim her: brothers, watchmen, kneaders, fate, and the society that sanctions them. Both, however, have the last word.

17

Conclusion

The Song lets its woman desire; the quatrain lets its woman remain

The Song’s woman reaches the last word by moving, wanting, and possessing: she seizes her lover, answers her brothers, reclaims her vineyard, and asks to be set as a seal on another heart.
Xuân Hương’s speaker reaches it by enduring: she yields her body to the water and the hands, and keeps only a vermilion heart, which no one can beat out of her.

For discussion

  1. Is keeping one’s heart a form of agency, or a way of consenting to the hands?
  2. Does the Song’s freedom depend on its genre, a lyric with no plot to resolve?
  3. The legend of Mỵ Châu blames a young woman’s misplaced heart for the fall of the nation. How does a heart kept “through so many waters and mountains” answer it?

Bible · Comparative reading

Rigged Contests

Reading David and Goliath through “Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh”

Albert Le Thanh Nhan
Sources: 1 Samuel 9–18, 31 and 1 Chronicles 10 (KJV); “Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh” and “Con Rồng cháu Tiên,” Ngữ văn 6, vol. 1

2

Starting point

Two stories, one troubled word: underdog

1 Samuel 17

For most readers, the archetypal underdog story: a shepherd boy with a sling fells an armored giant.

Vietnamese legend

Schoolchildren learn “Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh” as an explanation of floods that also “exalts and praises” the Hùng kings’ founding of the nation.

Read side by side, the two tales unsettle the word underdog. David is the obvious physical underdog, yet the only arbiter who matters has already chosen him. Thuỷ Tinh is Sơn Tinh’s divine equal, and becomes an underdog only through the terms a king sets.

3

Thesis

The underdog is made by whoever sets the terms

In both narratives, the underdog is made by whoever sets the terms of the contest, and each tale reserves its harshest judgment for the king who sets or resists those terms.

Saul falls for disobedience and for opposing God’s chosen one.
The eighteenth Hùng king falls even though he sided with a deity: his partiality breaks the pact on which his dragon-and-immortal lineage rests.

Neither anointing nor ancestry protects a king who rigs the contest.

Part I · David and Goliath

Two vantages on the same contest

Schematic, not to scale: the first panel shows the odds as Saul, Israel and Goliath read them; the second shows the arrangement the reader already knows from chapter 16.

Within the story, only David reads the odds correctly, and he announces the arrangement aloud (17.47). The mismatch is staged so that David’s victory may be glorious in the name of God.

Outmatched in body and unbeatable in theology, David is an underdog only from a human vantage.

Part I · The other loser

Saul, Israel’s own giant

  1. 1 Sam. 9.2“higher than any of the people” from the shoulders up: the very “outward appearance” God discounts (16.7)
  2. 1 Sam. 9.21Once a chosen underdog himself: a Benjamite “of the smallest of the tribes of Israel”
  3. 1 Sam. 15.23“Because thou hast rejected the word of the LORD, he hath also rejected thee from [being] king”
  4. 1 Sam. 17.11Saul and all Israel “were dismayed, and greatly afraid”
  5. 1 Sam. 17.39The king’s armor is useless to the chosen boy: “I cannot go with these; for I have not proved them”

The tallest man in Israel stands among the frightened, and his equipment cannot be worn by the one God has chosen.

In Samuel, chosenness is a relation that can be broken, not a possession that can be kept.

Part II · Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh

Thuỷ Tinh does not look like an underdog at all

The legend insists on parity. Sơn Tinh raises hills with a wave of his hand; Thuỷ Tinh, “no less talented,” summons wind and rain.

“One is lord of the high mountains [non cao], the other lord of the deep waters [nước thẳm], and both are worthy to be the Hùng king’s son-in-law.”“Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh”
“both young men please me”The Hùng king, after conferring with his highest officials

The king affirms the parity, then dissolves it.

Part II · The race

Not impossible, only slower, and in a race slower is enough

Sơn Tinh arrives first. Thuỷ Tinh never lacked the gifts. A list drawn from the land need not make the sea god’s task impossible, only slower.

Part II · The floods

Oán nặng, thù sâu: a heavy grievance and a deep enmity

The narrator frames Thuỷ Tinh’s response as rage and an attempt to “seize” Mị Nương, yet also names the motive behind the yearly floods.

Oán is resentment of a wrong suffered, and the king’s terms supply one.

Caveat This reading does not make Thuỷ Tinh just: his waters drown the fields and houses of people who set no terms.

What changes In collective causality, the origin of the annual catastrophe moves from a god’s temper to a king’s decree.

Even Phong Châu, the capital founded by the first Hùng king, seems to float “on a sea of water.”“Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh”; “Con Rồng cháu Tiên”

Part IV · The fall of kings

Only Saul’s fall is narrated and moralized

Saul

The Hùng king

His end appears in neither textbook tale.

  • “Con Rồng cháu Tiên” records a throne handed down for 18 generations under one title without change.
  • “Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh” opens on the eighteenth of these kings.
  • Vietnamese historical tradition makes him the last: the ruler whose Văn Lang fell to Thục Phán.

Part IV · The founding pact

He failed the pact his bloodline embodies

“Ta vốn nòi rồng ở miền nước thẳm, nàng là dòng tiên ở chốn non cao.”
“I am of the dragon race of the deep waters; you are of the immortal line of the high mountains.”“Con Rồng cháu Tiên”
“Kẻ miền núi, người miền biển, khi có việc gì thì giúp đỡ lẫn nhau, đừng quên lời hẹn.”
“Mountain folk and sea folk, whenever there is need, must help one another; do not forget the pledge.”“Con Rồng cháu Tiên”

He professes equal regard for both suitors, sets terms that favor the land, and turns the water into his kingdom’s yearly enemy. His bloodline did not fail him; he failed the pact his bloodline embodies.

16

Part IV · An objection

Was the king only sparing his daughter?

Objection

The founding tale itself declares land and water “hard to live together in one place for long” (“Con Rồng cháu Tiên”), so the king merely spares his daughter the separation her ancestors endured.

Reply

The founding tale answers that incompatibility with separation and mutual aid, not with a contest in which one side is quietly disqualified.

The king could have refused both suitors. Instead he disguised a preference as a fair race, and his people paid for the disguise.

17

Conclusion

A position assigned, not a measure of strength

Saul’s anointing does not survive his disobedience, and the line of the dragon and the immortal ends with the king who broke its pact. In both tales, underdog names a position assigned by whoever sets the terms, and severe judgment falls not only on the losing champion but also on the king who set the terms or fought them.

Read through “Sơn Tinh, Thuỷ Tinh,” the story of David looks less like the triumph of the weak than like a contest rigged by grace.
Read through 1 Samuel, the legend learned as praise of the Hùng kings becomes a record of the last king’s injustice, paid for by his people every rainy season.

For discussion

  1. If the terms of a contest make the underdog, who sets the terms in 1 Samuel 17, and who sets them in 1 Samuel 18?
  2. Thuỷ Tinh has a real grievance, yet his floods fall on people who set no terms. How should a reader weigh grievance against harm?
  3. Is a contest “rigged by grace” still a contest? What does the phrase claim about how 1 Samuel understands divine choice?
