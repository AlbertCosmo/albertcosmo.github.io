Facing the audience

AMany people would rather do almost anything than speak in front of an audience. Surveys in several countries have found that public speaking is one of the most common fears that people report. Even experienced teachers, actors and business leaders admit that they still feel nervous before they go on stage.

BThe feelings are easy to recognise: a fast heartbeat, a dry mouth, shaking hands and a mind that suddenly goes blank. These reactions come from the body’s ‘fight or flight’ response. When the brain sees a threat, it releases adrenaline, a hormone that prepares the body to fight or to run away. This response was useful for our ancestors when they met a dangerous animal. Unfortunately, the brain reacts in a similar way to a room full of people looking at us. Yet the audience cannot actually hurt us.

CWhy should an audience feel so threatening? One explanation is that, for most of human history, people lived in small groups and depended on them to survive. Being rejected by the group was dangerous, so we became very sensitive to what others think of us. When we stand in front of a crowd, we feel that we are being judged, and the old alarm system switches on.

DThe good news is that nervousness does not have to stop us. Preparation is the most obvious help. Speakers who practise their talk out loud several times, ideally in front of a friend, are less likely to forget what they want to say. It also helps to arrive early and check the room and the equipment. Speakers can then chat with a few audience members before starting, so that the crowd feels friendlier.

EAnother useful technique concerns how we describe our feelings. Many people tell themselves to calm down, but this rarely works, because a racing heart cannot be slowed by an order. In a study at Harvard Business School, people gave a short speech. Before speaking, some said ‘I am excited’ and others said ‘I am calm’. Observers rated the ‘excited’ speakers as more confident and more persuasive. Nervousness and excitement feel very similar in the body. So it may be easier to change the name we give the feeling than to change the feeling itself.

FFinally, it is worth remembering that audiences notice much less than speakers think. Psychologists call this the ‘illusion of transparency’: people tend to believe their nervousness is obvious to others. In fact, listeners often cannot see it. Most audiences want the speaker to succeed. A small mistake, a pause or a lost word is rarely as important to them as it feels to the person at the front of the room.
