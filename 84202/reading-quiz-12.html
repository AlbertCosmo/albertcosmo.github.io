Behind the perfect picture

AScroll through any social media app and you will see hundreds of faces that look almost perfect. They have smooth skin, bright eyes, white teeth and not a single spot. Very few of these pictures show what people really look like. Many have been changed with editing apps or with filters that work in real time, so the changes happen before the photo is even taken.

BEditing photos is not new. Fashion magazines have changed pictures of models for decades, and professional photographers have always chosen the best light and the best angle. What has changed is who does the editing. Today, almost anyone with a smartphone can make their face thinner or their eyes larger in a few seconds. Then they can share the result with thousands of people.

CMany young people say that these images affect how they feel about themselves. When we look at other people’s photos, we naturally compare ourselves with them. If the photos are edited, the comparison is not fair, because we are comparing our real face with someone else’s improved one. Some users even begin to prefer their filtered face to the one they see in the mirror. Doctors have reported patients who bring edited selfies to a clinic and ask to look like them in real life.

DSome governments have tried to deal with the problem. Since 2017, France has required commercial photos to carry a label if a model’s body shape has been made thinner or larger. In 2022, Norway went further. Now, advertisers and influencers who are paid to promote products must show a label when a body in an advertisement has been changed. Supporters say that labels remind viewers that what they are seeing is not real. Critics reply that a small label is easy to miss, and that most edited images are not advertisements at all.

ENot everyone in the fashion world is happy with the perfect image either. Some models have spoken publicly about how much work goes into a single photograph. It needs a team of make-up artists and stylists, hours of lighting, and then digital changes afterwards. They point out that even they do not look like their own pictures. A few brands now promise not to retouch the bodies of the models in their advertisements.

FExperts suggest a few simple habits for healthier scrolling. First, remember that a photo is a choice, not a fact: someone chose what to show and what to hide. Second, notice how you feel after using an app; if you often feel worse, you can unfollow accounts that make you anxious. Finally, follow people who post unedited pictures. Seeing ordinary skin and ordinary bodies more often can help us accept our own.
